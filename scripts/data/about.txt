WHO I AM

I am a May 2026 graduate of the University of Wisconsin-Madison with a
dual B.S. in Computer Engineering and Computer Science. But the short
version of who I am is this: I am someone who has been genuinely in love
with building things with code for a long time, who has lived across very
different parts of the world, and who tries, constantly, to learn the
next thing.


WHERE I COME FROM

I am an international person in the fullest sense. I hold dual Spanish and
Honduran citizenship, and I grew up across Honduras and Colombia before
coming to the United States for university. I attended high school at
Academia Los Pinares in Tegucigalpa, Honduras, and at Colegio Anglo
Colombiano in Bogota, Colombia, and then spent four years at UW-Madison
in the US.

Living in three countries shapes how you work. I am fluent in both
English and Spanish, and moving between cultures has made me genuinely
adaptable, comfortable joining new teams, working across differences, and
adjusting to environments that do not look like the last one. I do not
need things to be familiar to do good work. That multicultural background
is one of the things I value most about myself, and it is part of why I
am open to opportunities anywhere, in the US or in Europe.


WORK AUTHORIZATION

To be transparent about my status: I am authorized to work in the United
States on F-1 OPT, beginning July 1, 2026, and I am eligible for the STEM
OPT extension, which together provide work authorization for roughly
three years with no sponsorship required during that period. Beyond that,
continuing in the US would require H-1B sponsorship. Separately, as a
Spanish (EU) citizen, I have full, permanent work authorization
throughout the European Union with no sponsorship needed at all. I am
happy to discuss the details of either path at any stage.


HOW I GOT INTO THIS

I have been coding for a long time, and it started with games. In high
school I won an award for the best video game using RPG Maker, and that
early thrill, building a world and a system that other people could
actually play, is still the same feeling that motivates me now. It is why
so much of my project work circles back to games and interactive systems,
and why I care about building things people genuinely use, not just
things that technically work.

That early spark grew into a serious engineering path: embedded systems
and digital hardware design, applied AI, backend engineering, and
performance work. I genuinely enjoy the whole range, from writing
firmware that talks to hardware over I2C and SPI, to designing a CPU in
SystemVerilog, to building a retrieval pipeline for a RAG system, to
parallelizing an image codec on a GPU.

I dedicate a serious amount of time to this, well over 500 hours of
focused project and learning work, and honestly, in this stretch, I spend

to jobs. I have been working extremely hard on this job search, treating
it like a full-time effort, because I care a lot about landing somewhere I
can do real engineering and keep growing.

I also took my learning beyond the screen. I attended NVIDIA's GTC

HOW I WORK WITH OTHERS

In almost every academic project I have been part of, I have ended up
taking the lead, not because I sought the title, but because I tend to be
the person organizing the work and keeping it moving. And something I am
quietly proud of: I spend real time helping my teammates, even when it
brings me no direct benefit. If someone is stuck, I will sit with them and
work through it, because I genuinely believe that is how good software
gets built. I believe in collaboration, not as a buzzword, but as the
actual mechanism by which teams produce things none of them could alone.
My two years teaching Python to high school students come from the same
place, I like helping other people get better at this.
